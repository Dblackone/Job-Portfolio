# WEEK 1 — What BIM actually is

**Category:** BIM Basics · **KB:** L1.01 · **Level:** 1 · **Format:** Text post
**Posting slot:** Monday · **Suggested time:** 07:30–08:30 WAT (Tue 08:00 CET / Mon 07:00 GMT overlap)

## Post

```
Most people think BIM means a 3D model.

That misunderstanding costs projects real money, and it is worth ten minutes of your
time to fix.

BIM stands for Building Information Modelling. The international standard that governs
it, ISO 19650-1, describes it as information management across the entire life of a
built asset. Strategic planning. Design. Construction. Operation. Maintenance.
Refurbishment. Demolition.

Read that list again. Only one of those stages involves drawing anything.

Here is the shift that matters.

In CAD, when you draw a door, you draw two lines and an arc. The computer knows you
drew three shapes. It does not know a door exists.

In BIM, when you place a door, you record that a door of a specific type, size, fire
rating, finish and cost exists in a specific wall, in a specific room, on a specific
floor. That record can be counted. Scheduled. Priced. Clash-checked. And handed to a
facilities manager fifteen years from now who needs to know what to order when it fails.

The geometry is just how you see the information.

An example from practice. A client asks how many fire-rated doors are on level 3.

On a CAD project, someone opens the drawings and counts. It takes an hour, and the
answer is wrong by two, because a door was revised on the section but not the plan.

On a properly structured BIM project, it is a schedule filter. It takes ten seconds and
it cannot be wrong, because the schedule is reading the same doors the drawings are
reading.

That is the whole idea. One source of information, many views of it.

So the practical takeaway for this week:

Stop asking "do we have a 3D model?"

Start asking "what information do we need, who produces it, and when?"

That question is BIM. Everything else is software.

I am teaching BIM from first principles for the next 52 weeks. Next Monday: the five
things BIM is not.

Follow along if you are learning, or send this to someone on your team who is.

# WEEK 2 — Five things BIM is NOT

**Category:** BIM Basics · **KB:** L1.02 · **Level:** 1 · **Format:** Text post
**Posting slot:** Monday · **Suggested time:** 07:30–08:30 WAT

## Post

```
Last week I explained what BIM is.

Defining it by what it is not is more useful, because every one of these
misunderstandings shows up in real tender documents.

One. BIM is not software.

Revit is software. ArchiCAD is software. Navisworks is software. BIM is the process
those tools serve. You can own every licence Autodesk sells and not be doing BIM. I
have seen it. Repeatedly.

Two. BIM is not 3D.

A 3D model with no structured information is a 3D model. ISO 19650 is a standard about
information management — geometry is one carrier of information among several. A
schedule is BIM. A classified asset register is BIM. A beautifully rendered model with
generic families and no parameters is not.

Three. BIM is not a deliverable.

"Provide BIM" is not a specification. "Provide a federated coordination model at RIBA
Stage 4, plus an IFC export mapped to the agreed property sets, plus an asset data
register at handover" is a specification. If a scope says "BIM deliverables" and does
not name the information, someone is going to be disappointed and someone is going to
be unpaid.

Four. BIM is not automatic.

Placing a wall does not produce coordinated information. Naming conventions,
classification, parameter strategy, and process discipline produce coordinated
information. The software will happily let you build a model that meets no requirement
at all.

Five. BIM is not only for large projects.

This one costs the most money in practice. The coordination value is proportionally
higher on small, tight-margin projects, where a single unresolved clash can eat the
profit on the whole job. Big projects can absorb mistakes. Small ones cannot.

The practical takeaway.

Next time you see "BIM" in a document, replace it in your head with "the information
we need."

If the sentence still makes sense, it was used correctly. If it stops making sense, the
person writing it meant "3D model" and you now know something they do not.

Which of these five have you run into? I am curious whether number three is as common
elsewhere as it is here.

Next Monday: why BIM was invented in the first place.

# WEEK 3 — Why BIM exists

**Category:** BIM Basics · **KB:** L1.04 · **Level:** 1 · **Format:** Text post
**Posting slot:** Monday · **Suggested time:** 07:30–08:30 WAT

## Post

```
BIM was not invented because someone wanted prettier models.

It was invented to fix three specific, expensive failures that traditional delivery
produces reliably.

Failure one: coordination.

In a 2D process, the architect's ceiling drawing and the engineer's duct drawing are
separate documents. Nothing in the process forces them to agree. They are drawn by
different people, in different offices, at different times.

So the ceiling and the duct meet for the first time on site — usually at the moment
when changing either one is at its most expensive.

Failure two: information loss.

Design information gets re-typed at every handoff. From the model to the schedule. From
the schedule to the tender document. From the tender document to the order. From the
order to the O&M manual.

Every re-typing is an opportunity to be wrong, and none of them are checked against each
other. By handover, the operations team receives a set of documents that quietly
disagree.

Failure three: late discovery.

Decisions are cheap to change early and expensive to change late. Every project manager
knows this.

But traditional process gives you the least reliable information exactly when you have
the most freedom to act on it, and the most detailed information exactly when you can no
longer afford to use it.

An example.

A duct clashes with a beam.

Found in the model during design: an engineer moves a duct route. Two hours of work.

Found on site: the duct is fabricated, delivered, and half-installed. Now you need an
RFI, a structural check, a revised drawing, a fabrication change, a delay to the
following trade, and a conversation about who pays.

Same clash. Two very different numbers.

The practical takeaway.

BIM's real product is not a model. It is moving discovery earlier.

Every BIM practice worth adopting — federation, clash detection, model auditing, defined
information requirements — exists to move a problem from the expensive end of the
project to the cheap end.

When someone asks you to justify BIM, do not talk about 3D. Talk about when problems get
found.

Have you had a clash that was found on site? I would like to hear what it cost — no
names needed.

Next Monday: the 50-year history of BIM, which starts a lot earlier than most people think.

# WEEK 4 — The 50-year history of BIM

**Category:** BIM Basics · **KB:** L1.03 · **Level:** 1 · **Format:** Carousel (8 slides) + caption
**Posting slot:** Monday · **Suggested time:** 07:30–08:30 WAT
**Carousel spec:** see `carousels.md` — Carousel 02

## Post caption

```
BIM is not a new trend.

The core idea is fifty years old, and knowing that changes how you argue for it.

In 1975, Charles Eastman published a description of a prototype called the Building
Description System. It proposed parametric design and high-quality computable 3D
representations drawn from — and I am quoting the idea directly — a single integrated
database for visual and quantitative analysis.

That sentence, written in 1975, is essentially the definition of BIM.

In 1986, Robert Aish documented the term "Building Modelling" in a published paper,
arguing for both the concept and the technology needed to deliver it.

In December 1992, G.A. van Nederveen and F. Tolman published a paper in Automation in
Construction. It contains the first documented published use of the term "Building
Information Model." Their proposal was that aspect models from different project
participants combine into a single building reference model.

Read that again. In 1992, they described model federation. We are still explaining it
in meetings in 2026.

Then the software arrived. Charles River Software was founded in 1997, became Revit
Technology Corporation in 2000, and was acquired by Autodesk in 2002.

Then the standards arrived. The UK Government Construction Strategy of 2011 stated that
government would require fully collaborative 3D BIM as a minimum by 2016. ISO 19650-1
and -2 were published in 2018, taking information management international. Part 5,
covering security, followed in 2020. Part 6, covering health and safety information,
in 2025.

The practical takeaway.

When someone tells you BIM is a passing trend, you now have a fifty-year answer.

The idea did not fail for decades because it was wrong. It waited for computing power,
for interoperability standards, and for enough commercial pain to make the change worth
making.

All three of those arrived. That is why this is happening now.

Which of these dates surprised you? For me it is 1992 — the term is older than a lot of
the people using it.

Next Monday: BIM vs CAD, properly explained.


Sources: Eastman (1975), Building Description System · van Nederveen & Tolman (1992),
Automation in Construction · Designing Buildings, BIM Level 2 · BSI, ISO 19650 series

# WEEK 5 — BIM vs CAD

**Category:** BIM Basics · **KB:** L1.05 · **Level:** 1 · **Format:** Text post + comparison graphic
**Posting slot:** Monday · **Suggested time:** 07:30–08:30 WAT

## Post

```
In CAD you draw what a building looks like.

In BIM you describe what a building is.

That one sentence took me longer to understand than it should have, so here it is
unpacked.

Autodesk states the difference plainly in their own material: CAD uses a drafting tool
to create lines and arcs that represent a design, while the BIM approach uses actual
elements to represent real-world components — three-dimensional elements with parameters
programmed into them.

Here is what that means at your desk.

A wall in CAD is two parallel lines. It knows nothing. It does not know its height, its
material layers, its fire rating, or that it is a wall at all.

A wall in Revit is a wall object. It knows its type, its layer structure, its materials,
its fire rating, its cost, and which level it sits on.

Now change something.

CAD: you edit the plan. Then the section. Then the elevation. Then the schedule. Then
the details. Miss one and the drawing set now contradicts itself — and nothing will tell
you.

BIM: you edit the wall. Every view updates, because every view was only ever a window
onto the same wall.

Count something.

CAD: someone counts by hand, and the count is a snapshot that is wrong the moment the
design changes.

BIM: it is a schedule. It cannot be out of date, because it is reading the model.

Now the part most content leaves out.

AutoCAD is not obsolete, and saying it is damages your credibility with anyone who has
actually run a project.

CAD is still better for 2D detailing, site drawings, survey drawings, schematic layouts,
and fabrication details — anywhere geometric freedom matters more than structured
information. Most real practices run both, deliberately.

The professional position is not tribal. It is: use the tool that matches the
information you need to produce.

The practical takeaway.

If the value of the drawing is what it looks like, CAD is a legitimate answer.

If the value is what it knows, you need BIM.

What are you still using CAD for, and would you move it if you could? I am genuinely
interested — I still use it weekly.

# WEEK 6 — The real benefits of BIM

**Category:** BIM Basics · **KB:** L1.06 · **Level:** 1 · **Format:** Text post
**Posting slot:** Monday · **Suggested time:** 07:30–08:30 WAT

## Post

```
I am not going to tell you BIM saves 30 percent.

I do not know that it does, and neither does anyone repeating it. That number and its
cousins circulate endlessly in this industry, usually with no study, no sample size, and
no year attached.

If I cannot cite it, I will not post it. That is a standing rule here.

What I can tell you is the mechanisms — how BIM produces value, so you can judge whether
it will produce value on your project.

Mechanism one: coordination before construction.

A clash found in a model costs drawing time. The same clash found on site costs
programme, materials, rework, and goodwill. BIM moves the discovery earlier. That is the
whole benefit, and it is a big one.

Mechanism two: a single source of truth.

One model, many views. A plan and a section cannot disagree about a wall, because they
are looking at the same wall. Drawing-set contradictions — one of the most common causes
of RFIs — largely stop being possible.

Mechanism three: automatic consistency.

Schedules, tags, and quantities read the model rather than being typed. A door schedule
cannot silently disagree with the plan, because it is generated from the same doors.

Mechanism four: better decisions earlier.

Options can be tested while changing them is still cheap. This is worth more than every
other benefit combined, and it is the hardest one to put a number on — which is exactly
why people reach for made-up percentages instead.

Mechanism five: information that survives handover.

Asset data can flow into operations instead of dying in a box of drawings. Most projects
never claim this one. That is a choice, not a limitation.

The practical takeaway.

When you are making the case for BIM, do not quote a statistic you cannot source. Anyone
senior will ask where it came from, and "I saw it on LinkedIn" ends the conversation
badly.

Instead, name the mechanism and connect it to their project. "On a job with this much
services coordination, finding clashes in the model instead of on site is where this
pays for itself" is an argument. "BIM saves 30 percent" is a slogan.

Arguments survive scrutiny. Slogans do not.

Have you seen a BIM statistic quoted that nobody could source? I have seen the same
three numbers for years with no origin.

# WEEK 7 — Who actually uses the model

**Category:** BIM Basics · **KB:** L1.07 · **Level:** 1 · **Format:** Carousel (10 slides) + caption
**Posting slot:** Monday · **Suggested time:** 07:30–08:30 WAT
**Carousel spec:** see `carousels.md` — Carousel 01

## Post caption

```
The model you are building is not for you.

That realisation is the difference between a Revit operator and a BIM professional, and
it took me an embarrassingly long time to arrive at.

Seven people will use your model. Each of them needs something different from it.

The client wants confidence the asset meets the brief, and structured asset data at
handover. They do not care about your view templates.

The architect wants design intent, spatial coordination, and a drawing set that comes
out of the model without manual patching.

The structural engineer wants load paths, member sizing, and grids that agree with
yours. If your grids and theirs have drifted, everything downstream is wrong.

The MEP engineer wants to route services through the space that is actually left after
structure and architecture have taken theirs. They need your ceiling voids to be real,
not approximate.

The contractor wants buildability, sequencing, quantities, and site logistics. They will
ask questions your design model was never set up to answer, and the good ones will ask
them early.

The cost consultant wants reliable measurement tied to model elements. If your walls are
single generic layers, they cannot measure materials, and you have made their job
harder than the drawings would have.

The facilities manager wants to know what is installed, where it is, and when it needs
maintaining. They will inherit your model fifteen years after you have forgotten the
project.

The practical takeaway.

Here is the test I apply to every model I hand over:

Can someone who has never met me use this without asking me a question?

If the answer is no — if the views are unnamed, the families are generic, the parameters
are empty, and only I know why the worksets are structured that way — then it is not a
BIM model. It is a personal drawing aid that happens to be three-dimensional.

Build for the seventh person, not the first.

Which of these seven have you actually had to hand a model to? The FM handover is the
one I see skipped most.

# WEEK 8 — The model is not the point

**Category:** BIM Basics · **KB:** L1.08 · **Level:** 1 · **Format:** Text post
**Posting slot:** Monday · **Suggested time:** 07:30–08:30 WAT

## Post

```
I have reviewed models that looked spectacular and were worth almost nothing.

I have reviewed models that looked plain and were worth a great deal.

The difference was never the geometry.

Here is what a spectacular, worthless model looks like from the inside.

Views named "Copy of Copy of Level 1". Families called "Generic Model 4". Every door the
same type because typing the real ones was slow. No classification. No fire ratings. No
parameter strategy at all. Two thousand warnings, none triaged. Worksets named after
whoever made them.

It renders beautifully. Present it to a client and they will be impressed.

Now try to use it.

Schedule the fire-rated doors — you cannot, the parameter is empty. Extract quantities
for tender — you cannot, the walls are single generic layers. Hand it to the MEP
engineer — they cannot tell which ceilings are real and which are placeholders. Hand it
to operations — there is nothing to hand.

And here is the part that costs the most: nobody except the author can work in it. Every
question routes back to one person, forever.

The plain model does none of that.

# WEEK 9 — BIM maturity levels, and why ISO 19650 dropped them

**Category:** BIM Workflow · **KB:** L2.01 · **Level:** 2 · **Format:** Text post
**Posting slot:** Monday · **Suggested time:** 07:30–08:30 WAT

## Post

```
If someone asks what BIM level you work at, they are using language that the current
international standard does not contain.

That is not a criticism of them. It is extremely common, and knowing why is genuinely
useful in an interview.

Here are the levels, because you will still meet them.

Level 0 — unmanaged CAD.
Level 1 — managed CAD, in 2D or 3D.
Level 2 — collaborative 3D with data attached, produced in separate discipline models.
Level 3 — a single collaborative online project model including sequencing, cost and
lifecycle management. Never actually defined in detail.

This framework came out of the UK. The Government Construction Strategy of 2011 stated
that government would require fully collaborative 3D BIM as a minimum by 2016 — which
in practice meant Level 2 on centrally procured public projects from April that year.

It worked. It gave an entire industry a shared ladder and a deadline.

Then ISO 19650 arrived in 2018, and the notation "BIM Level 2" is not used in it.

BSI's own transition material states this directly: it is one of the most recognisable
changes in the move to the international standard, even though BIM Level 2 had been used
extensively in UK procurement and industry documentation. BSI and nima proposed the IMI
Framework in its place.

Why the change matters, and not just as trivia.

"Level 2" describes a maturity state — how sophisticated your setup is.
ISO 19650 describes an information management process — who requires what information,
when, in what format, and how it is checked.

The first is a badge. The second is a method. You cannot audit a badge.

The practical takeaway.

Learn the levels, because they appear in older tender documents, in job adverts, and in
interview questions, and you need to understand the person asking.

But do not write them into new documents. If a client says "we want Level 2", the
professional response is not to correct them — it is to translate:

"Understood. So we will need exchange information requirements, a BIM execution plan,
a common data environment with defined states, and a federated model with a clash
detection strategy. Let me set out what that looks like."

You have given them exactly what they asked for, in language that can actually be
delivered and audited.

That translation, done calmly, is what a BIM professional sounds like.

Has anyone asked you what BIM level you work at? How did you answer?

# WEEK 10 — BIM dimensions, and the disagreement nobody mentions

**Category:** BIM Workflow · **KB:** L2.02 `[CONTESTED]` · **Level:** 2 · **Format:** Carousel (10 slides) + caption
**Posting slot:** Monday · **Suggested time:** 07:30–08:30 WAT
**Carousel spec:** see `carousels.md` — Carousel 03

## Post caption

```
Almost every article about BIM dimensions states confidently what 6D and 7D mean.

They contradict each other, and almost none of them mention that.

Here is what is actually agreed and what is not.

Agreed, broadly:

3D — the coordinated geometric model.
4D — time. Model elements linked to a construction programme, so you can see the
sequence rather than read it.
5D — cost. Quantities and cost data linked to elements.

Not agreed:

6D and 7D.

NBS and Designing Buildings both record the same split. Some of the industry defines 6D
as facility management information and 7D as sustainability impact data. Others define
6D as sustainability and 7D as facility management.

Both positions are published. Both are used in real tender documents. There is no
international consensus.

It goes further. Dimensions above 7D — 8D, 9D, 10D, 11D, all of which you will find
confidently explained online — are not formalised or standardised at all. NBS also
records the argument that cost is not really a dimension in the first place; it is an
additional information field.

So what do you do when a tender asks for 6D BIM?

You do not guess, and you do not quietly assume. You ask, before you price it.

"Please confirm what is required under 6D — asset information for operations, or
sustainability and carbon data. The scope and cost differ substantially."

That single question does three things. It gets you a priced scope you can actually
deliver. It protects you from delivering the wrong thing at handover. And it tells the
client, without any showing off, that you know this ground properly.

The practical takeaway, and this is the professional position:

Specify the information, not the dimension.

Write "provide asset data in accordance with the AIR at handover" rather than "provide
6D BIM". One is deliverable and auditable. The other is an argument waiting to happen.

Dimensions are a useful way to explain BIM to someone new. They are a poor way to
specify a contract.

Have you seen 6D or 7D in a real tender document? I would genuinely like to know which
definition was intended — if anyone stated one at all.

# WEEK 11 — The BIM workflow end to end

**Category:** BIM Workflow · **KB:** L2.03 · **Level:** 2 · **Format:** Carousel (10 slides) + caption
**Posting slot:** Monday · **Suggested time:** 07:30–08:30 WAT

## Post caption

```
Modelling is one step out of nine.

Here is the whole BIM workflow, and it explains why "I can model, so I do BIM" does not
hold up.

One. Requirements. The client states what information they need, when, and in what
format. In ISO 19650 language this is the exchange information requirements — the EIR.
Nothing good happens if this step is skipped, and it usually is.

Two. Planning. The delivery team responds with a BIM execution plan: roles, software,
formats, coordinate system, naming, level of information need, the common data
environment, delivery milestones.

Three. Authoring. Each discipline models in its own file. This is the part everyone
thinks BIM is.

Four. Federation. Models are linked into a combined view. Linked, not merged — each
discipline keeps authorship of and responsibility for its own model.

Five. Coordination. Clashes and issues are found, grouped, assigned to a named person
with a date, and closed. Clashes found are not the output. Clashes closed are.

Six. Documentation. Drawings and schedules are produced from the model, not alongside it.

Seven. Construction. The model supports sequencing, quantities, and site queries.

Eight. Handover. Asset information is delivered in a structured format the operations
team can actually use.

Nine. Operation. The asset information model is maintained and used for the life of the
building.

Now count.

Authoring is step three. Everything before it decides whether the model will be useful.
Everything after it decides whether the effort was worth anything.

Most projects do steps three, four, five and six competently, then stop. Steps one, two,
eight and nine are where the money and the whole-life value sit, and they are the ones
that get dropped when the programme tightens.

The practical takeaway.

If you want to move from BIM modeller to BIM coordinator or manager, the route is not
becoming faster at step three.

It is becoming useful at steps one, two, eight and nine — the ones almost nobody is
competing for.

Learn to read an EIR. Learn to write a BEP. Learn what operations actually needs at
handover. That is the whole career path, and it is remarkably uncrowded.

Which step does your organisation do worst? Mine, historically, was step one.

Next Monday: ten things beginners get wrong about BIM — and it closes out this quarter.

# WEEK 12 — Ten things beginners get wrong about BIM

**Category:** BIM Basics · **KB:** L1.20 · **Level:** 1 · **Format:** Carousel (12 slides) + caption
**Posting slot:** Monday · **Suggested time:** 07:30–08:30 WAT

## Post caption

```
Twelve weeks ago I said I would teach BIM from first principles for a year.

This closes the first quarter. Here are the ten misconceptions that cost beginners the
most time — every one of them something I got wrong myself.

One. "BIM means 3D." It means managed information. ISO 19650 is a standard about
information management, and almost none of it concerns geometry.

Two. "I know Revit, so I know BIM." Revit is one authoring tool inside a nine-step
process. Authoring is step three.

Three. "The model is the deliverable." The information is the deliverable. The model is
one container among several, alongside IFC, schedules, drawings, and asset data.

Four. "It disappeared, so I deleted it." Almost never. It is view range, a temporary
hide, a filter, a workset, a phase, or a design option. Check in that order before you
panic.

Five. "I will set up worksets later." Worksharing decisions are cheapest on day one and
brutal in month four.

Six. "I will just explode the CAD to trace it." Exploding an import injects thousands of
foreign line styles, text styles, and fill patterns into your project permanently. This
is the most common act of lasting damage a beginner does to a project file.

Seven. "Detail lines are fine for that." Drawn lines carry no information and exist in
one view only. Legitimate for detailing. Not legitimate for faking model content.

Eight. "Warnings do not matter." Some do not. Duplicate instances silently double your
quantities. Know which is which.

Nine. "In-place families are quicker." Quicker today. Expensive for the remaining
eighteen months of the project.

Ten. "The model needs to be perfect." It needs to meet a defined level of information
need. Over-modelling is a real and expensive failure, and EN 17412-1 exists precisely
because the industry kept doing it.

The practical takeaway.

Every one of these is a shortcut that works today and charges interest later. That is
the shape of nearly every BIM mistake — the cost is real but deferred, so it never feels
like a mistake at the time.

If you are learning, the single most valuable habit is to ask, before every shortcut:
who pays for this, and when?

That is the first quarter done. Next quarter — thirteen weeks — is Revit, from opening
it for the first time to producing a documented drawing set.

what I write next.
