# 02 · Tuesday 4 August · Educational

**Hook:** A design change costs almost nothing in the model.
**CTA:** "Save this if you are planning a project in the next 12 months."

**Caption**

A design change costs almost nothing in the model. It costs a fortune once the concrete has cured.

This is the curve every project manager knows and most programmes ignore. The cost of a change rises with every week it stays undiscovered. Move a wall at concept stage and you move a line. Move it during documentation and you revise a drawing set. Move it after the slab is poured and you are paying for demolition, disposal, rework, delay, and the knock-on to every trade queued behind you.

Clash detection exists to drag that discovery as far left as possible. Running architectural, structural and MEP models together in Navisworks before construction documentation is issued surfaces the conflicts while they are still drawings. Service runs through beams. Duct routes against ceiling voids. Plant rooms that cannot actually fit the plant.

Across projects at Nu-Avenue I contributed over ₦10 million in savings, and a large share of that never looked like savings at all. It looked like nothing happening. No variation order, no stoppage, no rework, because the conflict had already been closed out in the model.

Prevention is the hardest value in this industry to sell, because when it works there is nothing to point at. The only way to see it is to compare against the projects that skipped it.

If you are running a project with separate design and construction teams, ask one question: who owns the coordinated model. If the answer is nobody, that is your exposure.

Save this if you are planning a project in the next 12 months.

# 05 · Tuesday 11 August · Educational

**Hook:** A Revit model is not a 3D drawing.
**CTA:** "Share this with your architect or project manager." (community)

**Caption**

A Revit model is not a 3D drawing. It is a database that happens to look like a building.

That distinction is where most of the value sits, and it is the part clients almost never get shown.

When a wall is modelled properly it is not a set of lines. It carries its type, its construction build-up, its fire rating, its area, its host level and its phase. Multiply that across every element and the model stops being a picture and becomes something you can interrogate.

So a schedule of every door in a scheme, by type and by floor, is not a week of counting. It is a view. Total blockwork area for a procurement enquiry is not an estimate. It is a query against what has actually been modelled. Change a window type in one place and every schedule, elevation and section that references it updates, because they were never separate documents in the first place.

The condition attached is the one everybody skips: garbage in, garbage out. A model built as decoration — geometry drawn to look right, with no types, no parameters, no discipline — produces confident schedules that are quietly wrong, which is worse than having no schedule at all.

That is what level of development is really about. LOD 300 means the elements are dimensionally accurate and reliable enough to build documentation from. It is a statement about how far you can trust the data, not about how detailed the picture looks.

Ask for the schedules, not just the views. The schedules tell you whether the model is real.

Share this with your architect or project manager.

# 06 · Thursday 13 August · Project Progress

**Hook:** We built the drainage before we built a single shop.
**CTA:** "Share this with anyone planning a multi-unit scheme." (community)

**Caption**

On the Oluku Ultra Modern Market in Benin City, the drainage was designed and built before a single shop unit went up.

That sequence was deliberate, and it is the part of market infrastructure most schemes get wrong.

Stage one was the reinforced drainage system — designed and constructed as infrastructure in its own right, not as a trench cut in around finished structures later. Stage two was setting-out, survey and the shop unit structures, positioned against levels the drainage had already fixed.

Run it the other way round and the market works for exactly one dry season. Then the first serious rain finds the lowest point available, which is now wherever the buildings happened to leave it, and traders spend every wet season standing in water with nowhere to go.

Water does not negotiate. It goes to the lowest point and it goes there every year, for the whole life of the scheme. On any development with hard paving and multiple structures, surface water is not a service to be added at the end. It determines where everything else is allowed to sit.

Setting-out is the other half of that discipline. Survey control established once, held throughout, verified before each pour — because a unit built two hundred millimetres off line is a unit that will never quite align with its row, and the error compounds along the terrace.

Infrastructure first. Buildings second. Slower to start, considerably less costly to finish.

Share this with anyone planning a market, estate or multi-unit scheme.

One accountable party usually costs less than two good ones.

That sounds like an argument for design–build, and it is, but not for the reason it is normally sold. The saving is not in the fee. It is in the space between the parties.

The standard arrangement: a client appoints an architect, the architect produces a design, the design goes to tender, a contractor prices it and builds it. Two competent firms, each doing their job properly. And still the same failure repeats — a design priced against assumptions the contractor never stated, a contractor building to drawings that were never tested for buildability, and a client standing between them holding the risk that neither one owns.

Every gap in that chain has a price. A detail that cannot be built the way it was drawn becomes a site query, then a variation, then a delay. A specification written without a live procurement view becomes a material that is not available in the quantity required. Integration closes the gaps because the person modelling the building is the person who has to deliver it. Buildability gets tested during design, not discovered during construction. # 11 · Tuesday 25 August · Project Progress

**Hook:** Six flats on one plot is not one project six times.
**CTA:** "What is the sequencing decision you always get asked about?" (engagement)

**Caption**

Six flats on one plot is not one project repeated six times. It is one structure that has to serve six identical programmes at once, and the difference shows up in sequence.

Six-flat residential block, Ikotun, Lagos. Delivered from foundation to completion — structural frame, column casting, suspended slabs, superstructure.

On a single dwelling you can sequence intuitively. Repetition removes that luxury. Every decision is multiplied by six, which means a poor one is not a small loss. Formwork that is stripped early to keep one floor moving costs you the cycle on every floor above it. A column set out slightly off on the ground floor is an error that gets built into five more.

So repetition demands three things. Verified setting-out held from one datum through the whole frame, because errors here compound vertically. Formwork cycles planned as a cycle — striking times respected rather than negotiated against pressure to move faster. And concrete control treated as a process with a record, not a delivery you accept because the truck arrived.

What repetition gives back, once the discipline is in place, is rhythm. By the third slab the crew knows the sequence, the cycle time steadies, and the programme starts predicting itself instead of being chased.

That predictability is the whole return on being slow and correct at the foundation.

What is the sequencing decision you always end up defending on site? I would like to hear it.

# 12 · Thursday 27 August · Design Inspiration

**Hook:** Clients do not brief in rooms. They brief in habits.
**CTA:** "Which room would you plan first?" (engagement)

**Caption**

Clients do not brief in rooms. They brief in habits, and the plan comes from translating one into the other.

A brief that arrives as "four bedrooms, a large living room, modern kitchen" is a list, not a design. It tells you almost nothing about how the building has to work. The useful conversation is the one underneath it.

Who comes to this house, and how often. Where does someone stand when they cook, and can they still see the children. Does the family eat in the kitchen or the dining room, honestly rather than aspirationally. Which door do people actually use — the front one, or the one nearest where the car stops. Where does the afternoon sun land, and which room can afford to take it.

Answers to those questions produce adjacencies, and adjacencies produce a plan. The kitchen sits where it does because of a sightline someone described. The guest room is by the entrance because of who visits and how long they stay. Circulation is short because someone mentioned carrying shopping.

Do it the other way round — arrange rooms first, justify afterwards — and you get a plan that satisfies the list and frustrates the family. It photographs perfectly well. It is simply tuned to somebody else's routine.

Across 10+ interior projects the pattern holds: the spaces people love are the ones planned around something they said in passing and did not think mattered.

Which room would you plan first, and what would you need to know before you did? Tell me below.
